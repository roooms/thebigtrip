---
title: "Waitangi to Ahipara"
date: 2010-06-08 02:26:00
author: "Dan Brown"
---
We woke up the next morning to heavy rain. As we were at a powered camp site we took the opportunity to charge camera batteries and the netbook, and take our time having breakfast. By about 11am the rain had cleared and one of the park owners stopped at our camper to have a chat. We talked about our plans to drive up to see Ninety Mile Beach and Cape Reinga, and he advised us to take a tour if we could afford it. There were a few companies who tour the area and depending on the tide drive one way (up or back) along Ninety Mile Beach, something we weren’t allowed to do in the rented camper. He recommended Harrisons as they had very knowledgeable drivers. We thanked him for the advice and set off in to town to pick up some more food before we drove to Waitangi.

Waitangi is where the much debated treaty between Maori and the British Crown was signed in 1840. The Waitangi Treaty Grounds had a 35 metre war canoe named Ngatokimatawhaorua (we couldn‘t pronounce it either) and some traditional Maori buildings. Our trusty Lonely Planet book said it was around $10 each admission but unfortunately when we arrived it had risen to $25 and we decided (having seen a lot of Maori culture in the Auckland Museum) that we should carry on north to Kerikeri, home to the two oldest buildings in New Zealand.

We arrived in the afternoon and it had clouded over a bit since leaving Waitangi, but the area surrounding Kemp House and the Stone Store was idyllic. There was a bridge over the Kerikeri River to the buildings, with ducks, geese and chickens roaming freely on the riverbank. The site for both buildings is in a natural basin with green park land surrounding it. Kemp House was viewable by guided tour only and these had finished for the day, but the Stone Store was still open and was selling traditional items like woven sacks, hand cut metal nails, knives, jars and cloth. In a second room it had resorted to more commercially viable products like ice creams and souvenirs. I liked the look of some Hudson Bay blankets as an extra layer in the camper van, but when I found out how much they cost I decided I could sleep in some more clothes instead.

The next part of our journey was off the well beaten Twin Coast Discovery path, out to a number of small bays on the east coast. First stop was Matauri Bay which was the main access point to the Cavalli Islands, where the original Rainbow Warrior had been re-sunk as a point of interest for divers. It was mostly untouched land, but it had been organised in to plots ready for people to buy a patch and build their house. Nobody had done so though and the whole area looked like it had been forgotten.

Over yet another hill and down in to a valley we found Te Ngaire, a popular looking place with lots of cute wooden bungalows. It didn’t have its own bay but didn’t seem to suffer the fact. It made Matauri Bay seem even more deserted. It lead straight in to Tauranga Bay, our destination for the evening as the sky was starting to darken and we’d read about a camp site right next to the beach.

We arrived and found a pitch close but not to close to the toilet and shower blocks, backing right on to the beach. Nobody was around and there was a note at the office saying find a space and we’ll see you tomorrow. Before the sun disappeared we had a walk along the beach and checked out the sea temperature. Bearable was probably the most accurate way to describe it. In the morning we got some more pictures of the beach and the camper parked so close to it. The sand was almost pink in colour and the sea went from a dark blue at the horizon to a light turquoise breaking on to the beach.

Our road left the coast for a short while as we continued north, finding it again at Mangonui, once a whaling port, but now famous for its fish & chips. We were in need of lunch so decided to stop there and try bluenose and chips. Along with kiwi style tartare sauce and plenty of salt and vinegar, it was delicious. Stuffed, we walked back to the camper and stopped at the information centre to see if we could use their phone to book a tour of Cape Reinga. The free phone number we had wouldn’t work on our mobile. Tour booked our next dot on the map was at the end of the Karikari Peninsula.

The Karikari Peninsula is a stretch of land jutting out from the mainland, with a ninety degree bend half way along it forming Doubtless Bay on its eastern edge. The bay gets its name from an entry in Captain James Cook’s logbook, where he wrote the stretch of coast was ‘doubtless a bay’. The shape of the peninsula meant that you had beaches facing north, south, east and west all within a few kilometres of each other. Perfect surfing territory the local guidebook said. Our one and only stop was going to be at the end of the peninsula at Matai Bay. The weather was looking good and as we pulled up in the empty car park we overlooked one of the two beautiful coves that create Matai Bay. It was a calm day, not a wave or surfer in sight. Not long after three more camper vans arrived, quickly demolishing our sense of isolation. They didn’t stay long though and soon we were on our own again. I pestered Nat to join me for a swim until she reluctantly agreed. The South Pacific hadn’t warmed up much between Tauranga and here so it was a relatively short dip in the sea. We really wanted to stay at Matai Bay for the night but were unsure how long it would take to get to the tour office the following day. Finally some clouds gathered overhead, making it a little easier to leave the incredible view we had and drive to Awanui, a little town north of Kaitaia.

The next morning we got up early and drove the short distance to Kaitaia where our Cape Reinga tour with Harrisons was to begin. Our driver, Allen was half Scottish and half Maori and had worked for the Department of Conservation at the lighthouse at Cape Reinga for three and a half years before leaving for Australia. On his return he began working as a tour guide for Harrisons and seemed the best person qualified to talk us through the history of the area on our journey.

The day started out very misty but soon cleared as we approached the beginning of Ninety Mile Beach. As the tide was out we would drive up the beach in the morning and head back along the highway road in the afternoon. Ninety Mile Beach is actually sixty four miles long and was named after the very rough calculations that it took three days for a horse and cart to make it along the beach, assumed to be travelling thirty miles per day. There was no accounting for the tide pushing the travellers further up in to the soft sand though, causing their pace to slow. It wasn’t until vehicles started using the beach, able to travel it in one go as the tide was out, that the correct length was measured.

We stopped a few times to take pictures and see the remains of a car that had sunk in the sand where creek water intersected the beach on its journey to the sea. Towards the end of the beach we turned inland and drove through quicksand (we were assured it was safe as long as you kept moving) up the Te Paki stream, back towards the main road. We stopped off at some huge sand dunes and Allen provided us all with sand toboggans to race down the dunes. The only drawback was you had to climb them first. After three goes I felt like I was going to pass out and thought how lucky we are to have chairlifts at ski resorts.

We visited a few other beautiful bays and enjoyed the lunch that was included in the tour, before arriving at Cape Reinga where the Pacific Ocean collides with the Tasman Sea. Collides wasn’t really the word to describe it on our arrival though. It was more like they were rubbing against each other. There were patches of white water and you could see the two different currents, but due to our fortunate sunny weather, there were no ten metre high waves. Maori believe when their spirit leaves their body, it comes to the end of Cape Reinga and jumps from the cliff top down to an eight hundred year old pohutukawa tree, where it climbs down through the tree roots to the earth.

On our journey back along the highway we stopped off at the most northern general store where everyone bought an ice cream. The tours must keep it in business as it seemed quite remote otherwise. The ice cream was great though and we happily boarded the coach again and set off for Rarawa Beach. The beach reminded us of Cannon Beach in Oregon, where the sand was bright white and so fine that it squeaked beneath your feet.

The last place we stopped was the Ancient Kauri Kingdom, a craft shop the size of a warehouse where the north’s famous Kauri trees are turned in to chairs, tables, plates, bowls, utensils, sculptures and one complete trunk was even hollowed to create the spiral staircase to the second floor. We picked up a Christmas decoration carved from forty-five thousand year old Kauri timber, not knowing whose tree it will be on this year.

After such a long day we decided not to try and drive too far and find somewhere reasonably close for the night. Just west of Kaitaia is Ahipara, well known amongst a select few as being featured in the film Endless Summer, an awesome tale of two surfers travelling the world chasing the sun and perfect waves. The campsite we stayed at was the busiest yet. As we arrived there was a huge circle of pensioners seated in very comfortable looking travel chairs on a large patch of grass near the site office. We chose a space where we could see the sun set, opposite a playground with a trampoline big enough for me to have a go. We finished a bottle of red wine we’d bought a few days ago and watched as the stars appeared in the sky.
