Also known as the Pink City (the old part of it at least) after Maharaja Ram Singh had it painted pink in 1876; a colour associated with hospitality, it was carried out to welcome the Princes of Wales (later King Edward VII).

Arriving after sunset, we ignored the rickshaw drivers trying to lead us away from the prepaid booth which offers a fixed price to certain locations within the city. We learned they were asking double the price of a prepaid ticket, so with our receipt in hand we climbed in to a rickshaw, joined by a local teenager with good English, who started the hard sell almost immediately. He was a local guide, could get us a cheap room, could organise tours and advised us that our choice of accommodation was expensive and dirty. He had a much better suggestion in a different part of town. Repeating ourselves over and over, we explained where we wanted to go until he relented and the driver took us to our chosen destination, Mirza Ismail road.

While unloading our bags from the rickshaw, the driver and his young assistant tried a few more angles to get us to part with some extra cash. They offered to take us to the Amber Fort, eleven kilometres outside of Jaipur, and when we explained we would take the bus for just ten rupees, they said it actually cost thirty, took over an hour to get there and suggested we might not be able to hold our bladder that long, so we should take his rickshaw and he would stop if we needed the toilet. I was amazed at their persistence and the last comment had me laughing out loud. We thanked them for the ride and began the search for a guesthouse.

A few places were full but we eventually found Hotel Pink Sun and got a room with a hot shower for a good price. It was down a small alleyway from the main road and surrounded by jewellery shops and small restaurants. These were however all closed as it was a festival day and everyone had spent the day on their roofs flying kites. We found a restaurant on the main road still open and grabbed some good food before calling it a night.

In the morning the shops surrounding us were all open and we walked through the alleyway, avoiding the coaxing of each shop owner to look around his store. Our first attempt at breakfast failed as after being shown a seat and having our order taken, someone emerged from the kitchen and explained there was no food available. We ventured on and after walking toward the Old City of Jaipur, stopped in the Indian Coffee House where the waiters wore long white outfits and little white hats. It had a slight colonial feel to it, but at the same time the building was small and cosy, and the menu quite reasonable. We had a good breakfast and wore the angry stare of the clerk as we left without leaving a tip.

From there we walked in through Ajmer Gate to the Old City of Jaipur; the original city was built within a large protecting wall and had been carefully designed with different areas for different services and bazaars. The city had since grown much larger and the sprawl outside of the Old City was the exact opposite of urban planning. We headed toward the centre where we would see the City Palace, Jantar Mantar and the impressive Govind Devji Temple. Along the way we browsed small stalls lining the streets selling anything and everything, until someone stopped us to ask why we (foreigners) ignore so many Indians who want to talk. The name he offered us was Hugh Grant. His real name was Khan and we explained as kindly as possible that maybe Daniel Craig would be a more popular choice if he wanted to impersonate an English actor. We also tried to explain, as honestly as possible that it is sometimes annoying and tiring to acknowledge every single person who shouts for our attention, be it to look in their shop, or ride in their rickshaw, or eat at their restaurant. We did point out that we frequently spent a large part of our day saying no thanks to offers, rather than just ignoring people.

After chatting for a while he asked us what we were going to see in Jaipur; he told us the main sights in the city were overrated and overpriced to enter, and we would be better off saving our money and meeting his family for a proper taste of Jaipur, and India. We had no concrete plans so we decided to trust Hugh Grant on his word and go and meet his family and fly kites with them as the whole city was doing it for the festival weekend.

Up on the roof of a three-storey house we met his friend Bharat and a group of kids who were all playing with kites. It was actually Bharat’s family home and his younger brothers and sisters, and his mother were all there as well. Khan explained that In India, your friend’s mother is your mother, your friend’s brother is your brother and your friend’s wife is… not your wife. We had chai and talked about a whole range of things, usually coming back to the subject of foreigners visiting places like this just to see the city sights and not engage with the locals at all. It was a recurring point that I could see they were a little annoyed with. Khan told us we should see some of the sights as it’s what travellers do, but we should return afterwards and join the family for dinner. He walked with us down to get a rickshaw and suggested a few places we should see that didn’t charge the exorbitant entry fee of the City Palace, and agreeing that we would meet him later we set off in search of decorated elephants, a floating palace and a monkey/sun temple.

Our first stop was a small distance out of the city, but we could still hear its noise behind us. Of the eight elephants normally at the site, only one remained as the others were being walked around the city on show (some of which we passed in the rickshaw not long after). We declined to have our picture taken with it as it would no doubt require some payment afterwards. We had our experience of elephants in Chiang Mai as a good memory and decided to leave it at that and move on. Our next stop was a floating palace, something I wasn’t expecting to see until Udaipur; but there in a lake to the east of the Old City was a beautifully symmetrical looking structure seemingly floating on the water. Our rickshaw driver spoke hardly a word of English and didn’t offer any explanation for the palace; and on looking for any more information in our guide book we found nothing. Our last stop was the Temple of the Sun God at Galta, around three kilometres east of Jaipur. Frequently called Monkey Temple due to a gathering of furry friends at dusk each day it could just as easily have been called Goat Temple or Pig Temple; it sat atop a hill offering views back across Jaipur and was reportedly a beautiful spot for sunset. We climbed to the top and I was amazed to hear the full noise of Galta and Jaipur reaching up to us clearly on the hill. Scanning the rooftops down below, they were full of people flying their kites in big family groups. Laughing and joking and general chatter all made its way up to us while we looked down at the activity below. We decided to head back before sunset, so as not to arrive back in the city after dark, and caught a bus going that way as our rickshaw driver had only been paid to drop us off.

Later that evening we met Khan at the Indian Coffee House where he told us quite frankly of his constipation and ordered a hot milk to try and ease it. We were going to make our excuses for not attending the evening meal we had been invited to as Nat was feeling unwell from a cold she had caught, probably from the chilly overnight train rides, and we weren’t feeling up to talking with everyone at an extended family gathering. Our excuses were not needed though as Khan explained that his friend had been called away and that he would no longer be going. He was very apologetic but suggested we join him for food the following day instead. Hoping we’d feel more up to it then, we agreed and headed back to the restaurant at our guesthouse for an evening meal.

The next morning we walked back in to the Old City and had breakfast at Mohan restaurant, recommend by Khan, before catching the number twenty-nine bus to Amber. We were going to see Amber Fort, built in 1592 by Maharaja Man Singh. The journey took thirty minutes and cost ten rupees, which made me think again of the rickshaw driver and his accomplice who tried to mislead us on our arrival in Jaipur.

We spent most of the day at the fort, an hour of which was taken chatting to a guy who looked Indian, called himself Australian, but was actually from Fiji. He was an interesting guy to chat to and we swapped stories of the places we had been and the struggle we sometimes encountered to get some honest advice or guidance. The fort itself was beautiful and interesting to walk around. There were many little side rooms and narrow hallways leading to small observation turrets or down to underground tunnels, offering escape should the fort come under siege. It was also such a nice, warm and sunny day that we spent a while just relaxing in the forts open spaces, soaking up the sun and people watching, occasionally being asked to be in photos. We realised not long after purchasing our entry tickets that we wouldn’t make it back in time to meet Khan, and without anyway of contacting him we just hoped he would forgive us, as he himself had highly recommended we see the fort whilst in Jaipur.

We caught another very crowded bus back to the city, most of it spent standing but without enough headroom to straighten myself properly. It occurred to me that it was actually better to have a crowded bus than an empty one, as the driving was so erratic it was useful that we were all squashed in so we didn’t fall about. We had a brief look around the Old City once more, checking out Nehru Bazaar, famous for its bangles, before heading back to our guesthouse and grabbing a bite to eat while preparing to make our way to the train station.

Our train was delayed just forty-five minutes from its scheduled departure, a new record on our travels so far. We climbed aboard and set-up our beds for the night, hoping the journey would be warmer than previous ones.
