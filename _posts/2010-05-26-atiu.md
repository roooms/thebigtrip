Coming in to land on Atiu after a 40 minute flight we already felt really excited. The Island looked really lush and although it still had a reef and beaches it was a much different lay out than Rarotonga or Aitutaki. The Island is smaller than the other two and the reef is much closer to the land which brings the crashing waves closer to you. There is an abundance of coral which you can see from the beach or rocky Makatea (old coral which formes most of the Island and has been pushed up from the sea over a long time, Atiu gets about 1cm higher each year!).

Anyway we landed at a very small airport where there were no customs officers just more Roosters to greet you! Luckily we were greeted by a guy called Marshall who welcomed us with the best looking Lei's (flower necklaces) we have had yet. We collected our bags almost out of the plane ourselves and then we were off in the truck to our home for the next three nights.

On the way Marshall stopped at a few places and explained about the areas, he showed us the harbour which is good for snorkelling and also a place called the Coral garden which is excellent as long as the tide is right and the waves not too big.

Then we arrived at his home, walking in definitely felt homely which being a homestay is obviously the idea!

Marshall showed us around the house and garden and also the Honda 90 which would be our wheels when we needed them.

Marshall's wife Jeanne is from Atiu and he is from England, they met in New Zealand and moved back to Atiu 17 years ago. He is now a tour guide and the best on the Island, Jeanne (who was away and so we didn't get to meet) is an artist and along with lots of pictures of their seven kids the walls are adorned with her paintings. She also sells cards, hand painted pareu's (sarongs) and out of a huge collection of really shiny shells they make keyrings. Marshall also make the most delicious sun dried bananas. They have a good thing going on here.

We looked around the garden and they had an abundance of star fruit, bananas, and avacados which we were told to help ourselves to. Lots of lovely flowers and plants, a couple of goats and yep you've guessed it, a Rooster!

Mashall showed us a female Fruit Dove who was sitting on a chick in one of his low bushes and we tried to get a glimpse of a bird called the Blue Lorakeet which had been introduced to try to stop them from disappearing and who are now thriving!

Later in the afternoon we grabbed the snorkelling gear and Marshall drove us back to the Coral gardens to see if we could get in for a look around. Unfortunately the tide was too high and the sea too rough but it was useful to go and check it out with him especially as he pointed out some sink holes in the reef which go down to 25m, if you get pulled in there I don't think you'd be getting out!

Spent most of the evening chatting to Marshall about all kinds of stuff, he is a really nice guy and obviously loves living here, I can already see why and we even asked about getting a flight back to Raro a day later but they don't do Sunday flights so just have to enjoy what we've got.

The next day we were up early to get breakfast and I made the most of having a sunny balcony to do a bit of Yoga on.

Then we were off on our first tour with Marshall, we picked up another couple who were from London and drove to the burial caves Rima Rau. These caves were used as a final resting place for the bones of warriors killed in battle hundreds of years ago.

I was a bit worried about it being creepy but it was just really interesting.

We climbed down into the first part of the cave and instantly it was very humid and earthy, there we saw the first lot of bones. There were stalagmites and stalactites all through the caves too. As we went deeper into the cave the tunnels got narrower and we had to crouch to get through some areas. There was a chamber we went into aswell which was a tight climb down but once there you could stand up.

We saw lots of collections of bones and skulls, some you could see had been involved in battles from the blows to the skulls.

It was nice to get back out into fresh air and we had a couple of hours to kill before our second tour of the day so grabbed the bike and headed off to the harbour.

On the way we stopped to take pictures of the shipwreck of a boat called S V Edna, it was a huge rusting piece of metal but looked cool as it was totally out of the water.

Snorkeling in the harbour was fab, like being in a big aquarium. It was quite calm as the entrance to the harbour took the beating from the waves. We saw loads of different types of fish, many that we had seen in Aitutaki. Also saw a guy fishing with a spear gun, goggles and fins, he had quite a collection of fish and a couple of Octopus.

I later found out not to get too close to the guys fishing like that as Sharks can be attracted to the fish blood!

Anyway all was well and we had fun trying out the underwater camera.

Next we were off to check out more caves which had a 30 minute walk to get to them. This was walking over the sharp and uneven Makatea (Coral) and through quite dense rainforest. We passed by a horrible smelling fruit which I cannot even start to describe as I don't want to think about it, but it's called the Noni fruit and is amazing at lowering cholesterol (Steve i'll send you some home!).

We arrived at the Anatakitaki caves and climbed down a ladder into the start of the first cave, it was amazing, full of huge Stalagmites and Stalactites. There were roots of trees called Banyan trees growing down through the caves looking for water, it was like a scene out of Indiana Jones or Romancing the Stone. Very wild.

The second cave we went into was the home of a bird called the Kopeka. It is only found here on Atiu and is quite special as it sounds like a normal bird when flying around outside but it nests in pitch black caves and so uses sonar to find it's way around the caves to their nests! We headed in with headtorches and could see the tiny birds flying around, they are so small their eggs are the size of your little finger nail. As we went deeper into the caves we could hear them using their sonar to find the nests. It was quite amazing as when we turned our head-torches off it was totally pitch black.

Our third cave contained an underground pool of fresh water so we all went for a very refreshing swim, actually it was just plain cold but once you were in it was nice. Plus you just had to do it, never know when we might get to swim in an underground pool again!

On our way back to the car we came across a fair sized crab called a Blondie who was asleep in our path, Marshall woke him up and he quickly scuttled away.

As our final part of the tour we were invited to go and join the local men in an age old bush beer drinking tradition called Tumunu. It is their time after work to relax and chat, usually female's are not allowed but tourists are!

We got introduced and then left there for about half an hour. At first it felt a bit uncomfortable just sat with six locals listening to them talk to each other and taking it in turns to drink from a small coconut shell cup. The cup was passed to and from the barman who sat in the middle and filled it up and passed it on to the next person in an anti-clockwise direction. I think they were giving me a small amount which I was happy with as it was quite strong! It tasted like a strong cider, quite nice. We signed a visitor book and then the main guy Papa Sam said a prayer, it was translated to us after and it was to welcome us and bless us. We were then asked to say our names and tell them a bit about ourselves, after this they all clapped and then we were asked lots of questions and were involved in the conversations. It ended up being a great experience talking to these local guys who work really hard and are intelligent and worldy wise, they all keep up to date with world news and knew more about govenment happenings back home than we did!

Our second full day on the Island involved us going out on the bike and investigating the Island by ourselves. It was really fun and as there were only about eight tourists on the Island and 400 locals we hardly saw anyone.

Our first stop was back at the Coral gardens to check if it was low tide, it was but the sea was a bit rough so we headed off round the Island, we stopped at lots of beaches and saw fish in the clear water, crabs scuttling all over the place. I collected some really nice shells and got some nice pics of a tame Kingfisher. We met a couple of fishermen who very kindly gave us a coconut to drink and told us about some football matches going on in the afternoon.

We stopped off at a beach where Captain James Cook had landed on the 3rd April 1777.

Next we went to find the sports field and were surprised to see hundreds of kids, all ages, girls and boys playing games of football.

We met one of Marshall's mates called Baz, a schoolteacher there and he said that all the kids in the Cook Islands had been given a pair of football boots and socks by Fifa to get them into sports and even funded a football stadium in Rarotonga. Most of the kids were still playing barefoot though!

It was getting late in the afternoon so we went back to the Coral gardens to have a final check for the day and again we were disappointed.

Last day and final chance to get to the Coral gardens, we were up early and off on the bike under a perfectly blue sky. We parked up and walked through the small bit of bush to get to the opening at the Makatea. We could already feel that the wind had dropped from yesterday so were hopefull and as we looked over the edge we were rewarded with a low tide and fairly calm waves. We climbed down and walked along through the ankle deep water to the area that Marshall had pointed out to us, the drag of the water was strong and I could see how if you were in the wrong place you could get swept into the sink holes.

The Coral gardens however were worth the wait, we saw mostly the same fish as at the harbour but also amazing Coral all different colours and shapes. We did see a different fish too, quite big with large dark eyes who kept popping out from under a rock to check us out. It looked a bit like a puffa fish and I later found out it was a Porcupine fish which blows up and gets spikey when disturbed!

After about an hour the waves started to get a bit rougher so we called it a day and headed back to get our stuff dry and pack our bags.

What an amazing Island, we have loved it here and really could have spent less time on the other Islands and longer here. It is wild and untouched with interesting history and culture. We have enjoyed feeling like we were the only people on the Island, exploring it and sitting on beaches where there was no-one else around.

The people are friendly and happy and although it would be great if more people visited it would be a shame to change the way that it is.

We got to the airport and joined the other few tourists that had been on the flight to Atiu with us. We were decorated again with gorgeous Lei's and waved onto our tiny plane back to the hustle and bustle of Rarotonga.
