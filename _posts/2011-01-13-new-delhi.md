---
title: "New Delhi"
date: 2011-01-13 19:56:00
author: "Dan Brown"
---
Landing at New Delhi Airport, the Captain informed us it was six degrees Celsius and very foggy. Having not really looked at the weather forecast for India, I was wearing shorts, t-shirt and thongs which had been very comfortable when wandering around the Sunflower Garden the previous night at Changi Airport. As we left the plane and entered the terminal the temperature drop registered and we knew it was time to get in to something warmer as soon as possible. Having collected our bags from the carousel we unpacked our jeans, jumpers, hoodies and shoes (things I so nearly sent home from Australia not thinking it through) and dived in to the airport toilets to get changed.

Feeling much warmer, but also dreading dealing with such cold a whole month earlier than we thought we’d have to, we changed our remaining currency in to Indian Rupees and caught a bus to the Paharganj area of the city centre. Walking up Main Bazaar Road we were swarmed with people trying to get us in to rickshaws, guesthouses, shops and travel agencies. We ended up chatting to a guy called Raj and took a walk with him to some accommodation he could recommend. The road itself was full of colour, smells and activity. Within seconds we had past the first of many cows we would see wandering around the streets. There were mongrel dogs everywhere, rubbish scattered all over the road, and small bonfires being lit every few yards for people to huddle around as the evening closed in. We got a room sorted in a crazy looking hotel with two awkward and cramped flights of stairs up to the first floor. The room was freezing and there was no hot water, but we were hoping to only be there for one night.

We had spent some time in Koh Chang with Mark & Sam planning our route around India but when it came to actually booking our train tickets we found that our first two routes were completely sold out. There was the option of taking buses instead, but having witnessed how people drive in India and experienced the non-stop beeping of every driver who has a working horn, we had made up our minds to catch trains instead. A saying we soon came to appreciate in India is, ‘anything is possible’. The solution as in most cases was ‘baksheesh’ (a tip or bribe). Train tickets are bought by staff working for the railway companies, knowing they can sell them at an inflated price to travel agencies, who then inflate the price further and sell them to people like us, who are inflexible in our plans and have less than one month to see as much of India as possible. We thought long and hard about whether we should compromise our last four weeks of the trip and change the route, or pay extra to make some seats suddenly free themselves on the trains we needed. In the end we stumped up some extra cash and got all the tickets we wanted booked and confirmed before leaving New Delhi.

That evening we sat in a small restaurant on Main Bazaar Road, opposite two elderly Indian men as the place was so cramped we had to share tables. The restaurant had no front wall, door or windows, it was just a man made cave opening directly on to the main street. Half way through our dosas we looked up to see two huge decorated elephants walking past on the road, shortly followed by two large camels. Scooters and rickshaws were dodging around them and beeping their horns; nobody else seemed to offer more than a glance at the four giant animals ambling down the road. It was a sight you wouldn’t ever see anywhere else, but in India seemed nothing out of the ordinary at all.

It was a relief to have everything sorted and one of the guys (John) working in the travel agency had invited us to join him and some of his friends at a bar across the road when his shop closed. We decided after a hard first day fretting over what to do, we should enjoy a beer before heading back to our freezing cold room. One beer turned in to a few and at the end of the night, one of John’s friends picked up our bar bill as she was celebrating the end of a work contract and was leaving India for Egypt the following day. Waking up the guesthouse staff to unlock the front door at 1:30am, we stumbled up the awkward stairs and met another guest in the hallway. He was a short, middle-aged Indian man named Captain Ashwimi Sharma (nicknamed Anju by his mother), and within a few minutes we realised he was really quite drunk. He talked and talked, first about his life and family, then about the universe and the stars, then about the creatures living in the sea, and how big the sea was! He wanted to know our family history, education, qualifications and future plans (actually a common talking point for Indians). We politely but firmly refused all the food and booze he was offering and securely locked the door to our room from the inside.

The next morning we didn’t have much time before heading to the station to catch our train. We decided to grab some food on the platform and once we had asked around and eventually worked out where to stand and what train to look for, found that our train had been delayed by an hour. That hour turned in to four hours and after several cups of chai we set-off for Amritsar; knowing full well we would get there in the middle of the night instead of late evening and have to try and find a place to stay by waking people up. Our first experience of the Indian rail network would be a pretty accurate illustration of things to come.

We met two very friendly families on the train to Amritsar; the first, a group of four who were sharing our compartment with us for most of the journey. They virtually forced us to eat the monkey nuts and sticky dates they had brought with them. The second, a mother with her daughter and niece, spoke a bit more English and we shared our biscuits with the two girls. They wanted to have pictures with us and offered us bracelets as gifts. We even got an invite to stay with them when we returned for our last few days in New Delhi.

The train arrived in Amritsar at half past twelve, nearly five hours later than scheduled. There was still a hoard of rickshaw drivers offering to take us to the best hotels in the middle of the night. We chose one with a hot shower and plenty of blankets on the bed, and tried to get some sleep ready for a day of exploring Amritsar and the Golden Temple.
