Getting in to bed at around 2am, we slept under four blankets on our first night in Varanasi. The next morning we said goodbye to Steve and Evan; they were off to find somewhere cheaper as they were planning on staying a while. On the rooftop terrace we had some breakfast and chatted with Haroon, one of two brothers running Elvis Guest House. He gave us a great history of Varanasi and the Ganges while our food was being prepared, and after we’d eaten we took an auto-rickshaw to see some of the sights in and around Varanasi.

Our first stop of the day was Benares Hindu University. Built almost one-hundred years ago, the campus sprawls over five square kilometres and has buildings dedicated to many skills from hairdressing to IT. Over fifteen-thousand students study at the university, and within the grounds is also the New Vishwanath Temple. We stopped off and removed our shoes to have a quick walk around the temple and take a few pictures.

Back on the (bumpy, chaotic and crowded) road again, we checked out two other temples, one covered in monkeys where we had to leave our camera in a locker at the entrance and one which we were only allowed to view from a distance behind a gate (since we aren’t Hindu). Sensing our driver Ved was running out of places worth taking us to, our last stop was at the Muslim Quarter Silk Workshops where we could see how the silk is dyed and woven in to amazing pashminas, shawls, saris and (especially for the western market) bed spreads. We toured the factory seeing how the dye is applied and then how special punch cards are used in machinery dating back to the fourteenth century to create the patterns seen in the silk pieces.

![](/thebigtrip/assets/images/2011/01/20222485-P1110739.JPG)

Nat really wanted to get a sari in India and we had read that Varanasi is at the heart of the industry. We sat with the owner of the factory and he showed us a huge collection of different styles and qualities. I must admit I was surprised to see Nat choose a colour and pattern so quickly, and along with a beautiful shawl we started working the price down for our two purchases. Settled on a price, Nat had her measurements taken for the blouse and skirt that would be made that evening to go with the sari and then delivered to us that night.

From there we headed back to our guesthouse, then out on to the Ganges in time to catch the beginning of the ganga aarti ceremony; a performance each evening as the sun sets. We walked from Jain Ghat near our guesthouse, along the riverbank to Dasaswamedh Ghat and after a great cup of chai, lit two small offerings to float down the Ganges and bring us luck.

The next day on Haroon’s recommendation, we got up at 6am to take a boat ride on the Ganges just after sunrise and witness Varanasi waking up and going about its early morning business. The only problem was Varanasi was cloaked in a thick blanket of fog that early in the morning and we could barely see a thing. Our boatman reassured us that very soon the fog would clear and we would have a great view of the ghats, but as we continued up the river the fog stayed with us, blinding our view but also creating an eerie mood we both quite enjoyed. Back ashore and still in the thick fog we conceded the boat trip hadn’t been a success and decided we would walk along the ghats for the rest of the day to see the life and activity they contain once the fog had lifted.

The fog did lift, about two hours later and we walked south to Assi Ghat and had some breakfast while watching locals bathing in the putrid water. Children were flying kites and playing cricket on the stone stepped embankment, women were washing clothes and soon the ghats were covered in drying laundry. We had considered heading twenty kilometres north of Varanasi to Sarnath where His Holiness the 14th Dalai Lama was making an appearance; but with the whole of Varanasi seemingly spending their day on the riverbank we decided to stay with them and people watch to our hearts content.

Cows and water buffalo were also common at the waters edge, making their way down from inside the maze of pathways during the day and laying in the sun or standing in the river. We walked for a couple of hours, back to the main ghat, Dasaswamedh and then hopped in a cycle-rickshaw to experience pedal power around the crowded streets of Varanasi. We headed back to the Muslim Quarter as Nat’s sari had taken longer to make than anticipated and it didn’t arrive the night before. It worked out well as she got to try it on with the tailor there to make sure it was a good fit, before wrapping it tightly in packaging paper ready for the journey home in her rucksack.

From there we got the rickshaw to Manikarnika Ghat, the main burning ghat on the Ganges and navigated our way through the winding alleyways past huge stacks of wood being used to build the funeral pyres for cremations. We didn’t want to get too close out of respect for the families carrying their loved ones down to be doused in the Ganges before cremation, but we did get a glimpse of the ceremonies and couldn’t avoid the thick smoke surrounding the ghat. The ash from the cremations floated on the surface of the brown water, slowly making its way down river where people were washing themselves or their clothes. Some people were even collecting the ash and drying it in heaps on the stone steps, for what we didn’t ask.

Cremation is performed on a body soaked in water from the Ganges, to remove bad karma from the body. We learned that although cremation on the Ganges is the most auspicious place for a Hindu funeral, five circumstances prevent a body being cremated. Instead the person is bound to a stone and ’buried’ in the middle of the river. Children less than eight years old were deemed too young to have created any bad karma; pregnant women were carrying a child which also couldn’t have bad karma. Snake bite victims were said to have had the bad karma removed from them by said snake, a connection with the serpent coiled around the Hindu god Shiva’s neck. Monks have no bad karma and therefore were not cremated; lastly lepers were ‘buried’ in the river according to the locals for fear of the disease spreading through cremation.

It had been an action packed day. Amazed at what we had seen and learned, we headed back to our guesthouse where our bags were stored and set off for the main street to get a rickshaw to the train station.
