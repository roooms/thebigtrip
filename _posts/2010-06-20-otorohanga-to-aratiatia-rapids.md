---
title: "Otorohanga to Aratiatia Rapids"
date: 2010-06-20 21:50:00
author: "Dan Brown"
---
Otorohanga had a small camper site that was cheaper than the more touristy Waitomo, so with that in mind we decided it was worth a stop. We checked out the main street and found most places closed or empty but the I-site was open so we popped in to see what else we could do in the area. We picked up some leaflets and drove across town to the camp site.

It was situated right next to the Kiwi House, a bird park in Otorohanga with various rare native breeds. The site was empty apart from the owners camper at the end which doubled as the site ‘office‘. We found out that our staying the night gave us ten percent off entry to the park as they were both owned by the same organisation. We spent the evening (and night) listening to the crazy wildlife just the other side of a wooden fence.

It had rained most of the night and wasn’t really easing up as we turned the bedroom back in to the kitchen/dining room. We had decided not to go to the Kiwi House as the weather was so bad, and instead just make our way to Waitomo to visit The Legendary Black Water Rafting Company and see if they could entertain us for a few hours.

There we met our guides, Vashti, Matty (who reminded me of a NZ Seth Rogen), Vee and Turtle who would be looking after us and ten others as we climbed down underground inside glow-worm covered caves and rafted along in an underground river. First task was to find a wetsuit that fitted (and had the fewest holes in it) along with boots and a helmet with a light bolted on it. They were all still wet from being cleaned (dunked in a bath of disinfectant) but we managed to get them on and huddle in a group for our first picture.

From there we bundled in to a van and drove out to the caves with a brief stop to pick up our individual rubber rings that we would float down the ‘black water’ on. It was here that Vashti told us that as it had been raining so hard for the past day and night, the water level was just shy of being too dangerous to enter the caves. But since it wasn’t at the limit quite yet, we could carry on and they would re-assess it half way through. We all had to find a ring that was the right size to sit in and then jump backwards off a small pier in to a very cold river to prove we could land in our ring and float off downstream.

Once everyone had tried it we climbed back in to the van and drove a short distance to the cave entrance where we had a quick safety briefing and then climbed down through a small opening rushing with water.

The next hour or so was spent clambering around in rapids anywhere between knee and neck deep. One of our first challenges was to squeeze through a tunnel that due to the water level only allowed six inches of breathing space at the top. Wearing a helmet with a light attached to the top meant you had to tilt your head sideways to keep your nose and mouth out of the water to breathe. It was great! We stopped occasionally along the way for more pictures (which we could of course buy at the end of the tour) and eventually reached the main cave where water was rushing through at an immense speed. We were set-off one at a time at the start of the stretch of water, and grabbed at the other end by one of the guides. In my case, Matty came to the rescue.

From there we had to hold the rings around our waist and jump down a small waterfall and form a line facing backward with everyone holding on to the person behinds feet (they called it ‘the eel‘). The current was really strong and they’d asked a few of us to go first to hold a rope at the start so we didn’t all drift off separately. Once we were all in a line the told us to let go and turn off our head-torches. We raced off in to darkness, only to be replaced very quickly with a ceiling covered in tiny green lights, glow-worms shining as brightly as they can, trying to attract food. It was an awesome sight, almost like looking up at the night sky on a clear night, but the stars all appearing so much closer. Every so often someone would turn on their light to glimpse at the cave shape and formation, before switching it off again so the glow-worms shone brighter.

The water slowed down and we all gathered in a group, grabbing hold of the cave edges to stay in one place. The next section was calmer and after a quick head count we floated down a narrowing section of cave, before natural light could be seen in the distance. A minute or so later and we emerged in what looked like wild forest (and it was still raining quite heavily). We climbed out and carried our rings up a small track and arrived at the river and pier we had jumped off earlier.

A short van journey later we all had to dunk our gear in the bathtub of disinfectant before we could enjoy warm showers and complimentary bagels and soup. We checked out the pictures but as suspected, due to the darkness and water distorting the lens, they were too blurry to be worth the cost.

We went straight outside to the camper and made up some sandwiches as we were still hungry, and felt instantly tired but knew we had to head on to our next destination, a popular place with a sulphurous stench, Rotorua.

As we arrived we drove beside Kuirau Park where there were rock formations billowing steam up in to the air. We parked next to the I-site and went in to see how much of the volcanic activity here could be witnessed for free. There was a leaflet for a nature walk along the edge of Lake Rotorua, and the mud pools and steam jets of Kuirau Park. Everything else was going to cost upwards of $35 each.

We found a nice camp site opposite a golf course and after parking up hopped straight in to a thermal pool to warm us up after a day in a freezing underground river. We planned the next day in Rotorua and stayed up until 1am to watch the opening ceremony of the World Cup.

Rotorua was wet and grey the following morning, but we drove to Kuirau Park where we found a Saturday market in full swing. The mud pools and steaming holes in the ground were mainly well fenced in, but there were a number of new ones opening up that were cordoned off with yellow tape. The Department of Conservation must have their hands full trying to keep the area safe.

We made our way a bit hastily around the nature walk (the smell wasn’t something I was going to miss once we’d left), then decided to make our way out of Rotorua to a less busy thermal park called Orakei Korako. We wouldn’t be able to do it on the same day, but they offered free camping for visitors so we jumped at the chance to save a bit of cash.

When we got there we realised the ‘free camping’ was actually just an invitation to stay in their car park overnight. They did have toilets though or we would have had to drive to Taupo about thirty minutes away. We were told cheerily by the guy working there that the rain was staying until Wednesday so we would get soaked tomorrow, but it would be worth it. The thermal valley was the other side of a river that we would be ferried across in the morning. It looked like a glacier creeping down through the hills to the waters edge, but it was steaming constantly. We settled in for the night, wrapped in our snowboarding thermals as we had no power hook-up to make the heater work.

The next morning we had a bit of rain then it looked to clear. We were the only ones there so we got to have an unexpected boat ride to warm it up before the lake crossing. It was the same guy as the evening before but we forgot to get his name. He said he nearly came and asked last night if we wanted to join him for a few cold ones and warm up in a thermal pool but thought we were already asleep. That would have been great because we were wrapped up in multiple layers trying to stay warm.

The river was dotted with wake board jumps and rails and we found out the business owners son is the best wake boarder in New Zealand. It gets no use this time of year though. As the boat turned around and headed back up the river to the drop-off jetty, the heavens opened and it started to pour down. We were handed a small umbrella (luckily we both had our coats on anyway) as we stepped of the boat and made our way up the boardwalk that had been built to guide you around the area.

The first thirty minutes we spent trying to shield the camera from the rain whilst taking pictures, it was pretty miserable. But the clouds passed again and the sun came out. We got some great pictures of what looked like another planet. There were geysers (one called Diamond Geyser which may be lost on the Kiwi’s) and mud pools and thermal caves. The weather stayed dry and as we completed our loop of the boardwalk we went back around a small section again to get better pictures. Back on the jetty we pressed a little buzzer and a minute or so later we heard the boat start-up on the other side of the lake.

As we headed back to our camper Nat spotted one of the geysers shooting boiling water and steam high up in to the sky. We couldn’t be sure if it was one we passed on the trail, but either way we got to see one erupt, even though it was from a distance.

We had a few more stops planned before finding a place to stay in Taupo, first of which was Huka Falls where a huge river powers through a narrow stretch of rock before it is released again in to a wider section. We couldn’t afford to take a jet boat ride to it, but did see one arrive whilst we were there. It raced through the white water and spun around a few times before heading back the way it came. Although it isn’t a high waterfall, the sheer volume of water makes it just as impressive.

Next we stopped of at The Honey Hive where we got to see bees inside a glass hive and taste ten or so different flavoured honeys. We chose a Manuka honey to go on our toast or in our porridge now we’re having warm breakfasts in the morning.

The last stop of the day was at Aratiatia Rapids where the same river had been halted by a dam that opens three times a day and floods the area below it. We arrived just before 2pm and made our way to the viewpoint just in time to hear the sirens warning everyone the dam was about to open. The valley below the lookout was deep and full of jagged rocks, but as the dam opened water started rushing down passed us, the level slowly rising. Within five minutes the water must have been twenty feet deep.

Satisfied we’d seen everything worth seeing (at no cost) between Rotorua and Taupo, we drove on with a plan to spend the night in Taupo and look around it the next day.
