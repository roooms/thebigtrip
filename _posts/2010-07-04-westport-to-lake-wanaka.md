We decided early on our first night in Westport that we should stay a second night, giving us a chance to catch up on life at home now the phone had signal and internet access was the cheapest we’d seen in NZ. It would also allow us to get two good nights sleep in a warm campervan as we had power. Free showers just topped the whole stay off perfectly!

Our travelling bible (also known as Lonely Planet) gave Westport a pretty dire review, but it did mention a small brewery that did free beer tasting, and a decent café, at opposite ends of the main street.

We awoke the next morning to brilliant sunshine. There was a slight chill in the air early on, but with a hot shower, a big bowl of porridge and a very rough plan for the day, we walked right through the heart of Westport, from the holiday park to the main commercial area the other side of town. The main street was lined on both sides with small shops, everything a town could need to be self-sufficient. We looped up one side then back down the other, looking for nothing in particular. There was a small fundraiser taking place for St John Ambulance, but other than that it must have been a regular, quiet Saturday morning in a town that doesn’t see many travellers, probably due to the LP right-up.

Driven forward by the chance to sample good coffee and cake, we made our way to Currtino’s Yellow House Café, stopping on the way to ring Virgin Atlantic from a callbox and push back our Delhi-London flight to February 21st 2011. Having been advised each amendment would incur a fee by Trailfinders, we were very happy when Virgin told us the change was free. We celebrated with incredible carrot cake and big lattés.

Westport sits alongside the Buller River and so after our refreshments I decided it would be nice to walk back in to town along the waters edge. It turns out the river is a pretty dull stretch of murky water, wide, shallow and calm. We took the next road we could back on to the main street and carried on until we arrived at West Coast Brewing Company. We (and by that I mean mainly I) sampled three ales and bought four small bottles. The lady working there was covering for her daughter but gave us a wicked beer bread recipe we will have to try out soon, and recommended we head to Tauranga Bay just a few kilometres out of town to see the seal colony there.

We made our way back to the campsite, packed up the camper for the short drive to Tauranga Bay, and set off to the coast. It was still sunny and a nice day to be watching the seals playing down on the rocks below the lookout. We also saw our first wild weka bird clambering through the shrub beside the walkway.

We chilled out for the evening in the camper and set off the next morning along the coast road, with a view to staying at a DoC site near Hokitika the following night. Lonely Planet rates the West Coast SH6 as one of the planets ten best road trips so we had high hopes having driven the Californian Coast Highway only a few months before. Along the way we stopped at Punakaiki to see Pancake Rocks, a weird layering formation of the coastal rock through a process called stylobedding.

At Hokitika we dropped in to the I-site to see what was on offer, had a wander along the black sand beach before a bite to eat, then before dark, drove the short hop out of town to the DoC camp site at Lake Mahinapua. The lake was completely still, giving a great opportunity to capture a mirrored image of the mountains rising high behind it. It quickly chilled down as the sun disappeared and other than a few cars arriving later to spray grass everywhere whilst doing donuts in one of the camps fields, we were left in peace.

The next morning we went back in to Hokitika as it is one of the best places to buy Pounamu/Greenstone/Jade in New Zealand. We checked out quite a few of the shops and Nat found a nice pendant. We grabbed some supplies for the next few days as we were heading in to glacier country, and drove further south to another DoC site just outside of Franz Josef Glacier.

The DoC leaflet was a bit generous in the description of the site and when we finally found it after missing the smallest of signs twice, it was just a car park with a toilet in the corner. As it was getting dark we decided it would do. Another night without power and we were starting to feel the cold; we both woke up early and I decided to drive down to a spot by the lake we thought we’d be overlooking with Nat still tucked up in bed in the back of the camper. We had breakfast there and drove on to the village of Franz Josef, arriving somewhere nice and early for once.

This arrival in a place with a population of more than a few possums and birds meant we had phone signal just in time for Judy to call. I disappeared inside an internet café while Nat had chance to catch up with her mum, before we drove on to the beginning of the Franz Josef Glacier walk.

It was a gentle but long walk up hill to the glacier. It was one of those things you really feel like you should do but aren’t sure why. We arrived at the terminal face and were greeted with a mass of ice, an impressive sight but leaving you unsure what to do next. We weren’t allowed within about two-hundred metres of the ice as it was deemed too dangerous. The only way to get closer was on a guided tour, something we couldn’t justify splashing out on. Helicopters buzzed overhead, full of people who could afford to land on top of the glacier and have a wander round before being whisked back to a warm chalet somewhere.

We sat next to one of the many impressive waterfalls lining the valley we had just walked through and chilled out for a few minutes. On taking a second look I decided it would be possible to climb up the side of the waterfall, and with a ‘here he goes again’ look from Nat I hopped across some rocks in the pool at the bottom of the falls and started eyeing up how I could climb it. After a tricky climb that I was very pleased with I got Nat to take a picture before I started climbing down again. Getting up there was hard and I knew it would be harder getting down. A few feet from the bottom, trying to find the tiniest of footholds right at water level, my grip gave out and I slipped down in to the water. Managing to stay on my feet the water was just under knee deep, but unsurprisingly it was icy cold and made the forty-five minute walk back to the campervan a bit more interesting.

With a change of clothes sorted in the campervan, we drove on to the next glacier, Fox. Thankfully it was a much easier and shorter walk to Fox Glacier, and it was much more impressive. We walked around ponds of water turned cloudy blue by rock sediment and hopped across stepping stones that had been placed to aid river crossings. Arriving at the terminal face we soaked in the scene and took a few pictures, before heading back to the van pleased with the amount of walking we had done in the day.

We spent the night in Fox at the Fox Glacier Lodge. It was a cosy little place with nearly every site available. We parked up and were ecstatic to find the showers were free. We had read that one of the bars in Fox was a good place to spend the night, so we wandered in around 9pm to see what was going on. The main street was dark and quiet, but as we neared the bar we could hear music and a hustle of people inside.

We grabbed some drinks, sat down and within a few minutes had met a few of the Fox locals and also some New Zealanders travelling by campervan to see more of their own country, what a novel idea! We got a tip off by a guy called Ben - whose well-beaten, very crooked nose and rugby player physique, hid his friendly personality - that a salmon farm on the road we would be taking south in the morning was a must for the chowder available. The recommendation was slightly biased as ‘they’ were his parents. He was a farmer and had lived in Fox his whole life. We finished the evening with shots of Chartreuse, bought by a guy called Joe, who seemed to be the last man standing in a group of friends who were now too drunk or missing to make it to the bar. We made it back to the camper and had a good nights sleep, probably due to the shot.

In the morning we drove to Lake Matheson, just outside of Fox. It was well known for being a great picture spot when the lake was still as it reflected the mountain range behind it. The walk around it was nice and we got some ok pictures, but were defeated by the ducks and swans who decided to create masses of ripples in the water just in front of the viewpoint. Another viewpoint four kilometres on from the lake gave the best opportunity to see the Fox Glacier from a distance. It was framed by a mountain either side, and you got a much better idea of its size even though it was the furthest point we‘d seen it from.

We cruised back through Fox and out the other side, continuing down the West Coast until we stumbled upon the Salmon Farm Café & Shop on the Paringa River. We had a bowl of amazing salmon chowder each and picked up a huge apple and raspberry muffin for later. On the way out we thanked the owner and said we had been referred by her son, but neglected to mention it was in a pub only twelve hours previously.

The weather was great again and although we’d planned to drive back inland through the Haast Pass to Wanaka for our next night, Nat really wanted to be by the coast while the weather was so good, and we decided to drive off the main route and down to Jackson Bay. It had a restaurant called The Cray Pot where we could fulfil our crayfish craving since we’d missed out in Kaikoura.

It was a picturesque little place with a long pier stretching out in to the harbour. There were no more than ten our so houses in the area and we only passed two people as we drove in and found a space to park. The Cray Pot was understandably closed as we were probably two of a handful of visitors Jackson Bay sees during the winter months. We had a walk along the pier and looked out at the Southern Alps across the water, before deciding we could still get back on schedule and make it to Wanaka before nightfall.

We drove back to the main West Coast road and headed inland, climbing up to the Haast Pass at 563 metres above sea level. On the way we passed over hundreds of creeks and alongside many waterfalls, most only a few feet from the winding road hugging in to the mountains edge. As we emerged from the other side of the Southern Alps it was getting dark and we didn’t see much of the view as the road took us between Lake Wanaka and Lake Hawea. We settled on a holiday park just north of Wanaka and drove the few kilometres down its dusty unsealed track in the darkness, not knowing what to expect at the end. Thankfully it was a warm welcome and more free showers so we found ourselves a spot and looked forward to the thought of seeing Wanaka in the morning.
