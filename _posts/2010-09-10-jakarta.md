We were staying at the Hotel Tator on Jalan Jaksa, in a pokey room on the third floor. It came with a fan that worked perfectly if you wedged a piece of wood down beside the on switch. Just before dawn the call to prayer began. Depending on how close we are to a mosque we can sometimes sleep through it now. We went downstairs for a breakfast of toast and eggs with thick black Javanese coffee, before starting out towards Lampangan Merdeka (Freedom Square), about a fifteen minute walk away.

I was surprised to see such an open space - almost one kilometre square - in the middle of Jakarta, having heard frequently about its sprawling grey sea of houses and polluted streets. We paid almost seventy pence each to gain entrance to the National Monument and squeezed in to the lift which took us to the observation deck. At one-hundred and thirty-two metres above the ground we could see almost three miles in any direction before the smog made it difficult to make out buildings in the distance. There were helpful pictures of what you should be able to see on a good day - I guess after a week of no traffic - with numbers next to buildings and explanations of what was what.

To the east of Freedom Square is Gambir Station where we hoped to by cheap tickets to Yogyakarta for the following day. Having worked out we needed to fill in a slip requesting our chosen train and time, we queued at the ticket desk only to find the train was fully booked already. That particular train - the cheap one - was fully booked for the next ten days, a consequence of Jakartans heading home towards the end of Ramadan to celebrate with friends and family. There was however plenty of seating available on the ‘argo’ (luxury) train which left at a more sensible time but cost twice as much. We retired to Dunkin’ Donuts opposite the ticket desk for light refreshment whilst we considered our options.

In the end we decided we had to keep moving to see all we had planned before our return to Australia, so we bought to argo tickets and made our way out to hop on the TransJakarta bus network to get to Kota - three miles north of Gambir - previously the Dutch capital of Batavia.

Kota had an old colonial feel to it. We walked around the small square and found the last remaining Dutch drawbridge - named Chick Market Bridge, flanked by a concrete replacement and a train bridge spanning the canal. The canal looked like this morning’s Javanese coffee, thick black water; you could see it being stirred up as single seat paddle boats made their way back and forth.

On the way back to the square we decided to treat ourselves to a drink in Café Batavia; a favourite of Winston Churchill, who has a bar named after him on the first floor. We made the most of it, nursing a drink each for around an hour whilst we enjoyed the art-deco furnishings and checked out the walls covered in pictures of the rich and famous.

Having experienced Jakarta’s incredibly popular new bus service (and how incredibly busy it is) we opted for the train back from Kota to Gambir. It was a little cheaper but we soon realised doesn’t ever operate to its schedule, and was running nearly forty-five minutes late. It was definitely worth the experience though. Our whole train carriage seemed to know each other and the friendly atmosphere had people laughing and joking non-stop. There were hardly any seats so as to allow the maximum capacity of people standing, but every now and then someone would squeeze past with a little cart full of chicken satay and fried bean curd seeing who was interested. As we left the station, none of the doors closed and people were hanging out the sides smoking cigarettes. It was an elevated rail network which peaked above most of the roof-tops so we had a great view of the city as it got dark. At the next station we saw ten or twenty people climb on to the roof of the train before it set off again. One of the locals in the carriage explained that you can ride for free on the roof and it is not policed. Arriving at Gambir I had the camera ready as we fought with people trying to get on the train while we got off. I spun around to take a picture of the few crazy enough to ride on the roof, and found the whole carriage top crammed with people.

![](/thebigtrip/assets/images/2010/09/14855777-P1060212.JPG)

As we started the fifteen minute walk back to Hotel Tator it started to rain. Within minutes it was a falling torrent and the roads were two inches deep in water, thanks to Jakarta’s rubbish filled drains and sewers. We waited under shelter for a few minutes but realised it wasn’t going to subside, and in the end just went for it, getting back to the hotel soaked through but soon warm and dry.

![](/thebigtrip/assets/images/2010/09/14855779-P1060214.JPG)

That evening we went to a recommended satay restaurant and had the best chicken satay of our trip so far. We picked up some snacks for the train journey in the morning before getting back to our hotel and starting the packing.

In the morning we got the same breakfast of toast and eggs and grabbed a Bajaj (Indonesian tuk-tuk) to Gambir Station. Our luxury train arrived and we were greeted with reasonable sized seats, air-con and even little pillows if you wanted to get some sleep on the eight hour journey. Mid-way through we were even served hot food although that wasn’t included in the price.

The journey didn’t disappoint and the transition from Jakarta city to the open expanses of paddie fields in the remote towns we passed through was really interesting. Jakarta for us didn’t meet with our expectations of a cramped, polluted, greyness as far as the eye could see, but I guess we were only there for a day in the city centre, focusing on the highlights.
