We had a bit of trouble as we weren't going straight to India (few weeks in Thailand first) so they wanted to know why we needed more than a month visa for our 4 week stint in India. Hopefully you won't have this issue if you go from Cambodia straight to India, or only have a few days in between. Originally they said we had to apply in Bangkok, our last stop before India but we explained we wouldn't be there long enough (5 day process in Thailand). We only got a three month visa even though the application form says six months. The guy at the embassy explained that they only give you what you need so if you can prove you need six months (maybe with flight itinerary's or something) then I can't imagine them giving you less. The price is the same for anything up to six months so it should be around 55 USD as a tourist. A guy in front of us in the queue mentioned he would be volunteering in an effort to speed up the process and it increased the price to nearly 100 USD so avoid that if you are volunteering at all. It takes three working days after your application so you will need to be in Phnom Penh long enough. I hope that helps. Let me know if you need any more info. Thanks for the comments. **Friday 10th December** Back on the mainland we met Jenna from Finland. She was also travelling to Phnom Penh but hadn’t got any US Dollars handy for the border crossing. We had heard that the Cambodian Riel was in use throughout the country, but the US Dollar was widely accepted and frequently a mixture of both made up a transaction. We had some spare and so ended up travelling together to the border.

At the border we had to pay $2 to get our exit stamp out of Laos, and then walk the hundred-or-so metres through the border void before arriving in Cambodia. We first had to visit a small marquee where we were advised of the possible illnesses you could get in Cambodia, and had our temperatures taken (which cost a $1). From there we went to the visa application hut and filled out the forms and handed over our passport photos that now look nothing like us. That was another $23. We took our passports with our shiny new visas to the border arrivals hut and paid $1 to get stamped in to Cambodia. We were in!

We had to wait for enough people to cross the border to fill our bus, so we sat at some makeshift food stalls where we bought some snacks and drinks, and tried to forget all the Lao we had learned while repeating the Khmer phrases we would be using until we made it back in to Thailand. It took a while but eventually we climbed aboard a baking hot bus and found that there were actually too many people. Out came small plastic seats and an unlucky few had to perch on them in the central aisle as we left the border. Behind us sat Jenna and our soon to be new friend Magnus, a Scot from the Isle of Skye who was cycling around South East Asia on a purpose built bike that he had just been charged another $15 to load on the bus.

As we stopped off at various places along the route, the bus finally shed enough passengers so everyone got a proper seat. We bumped along dirt roads and the bus driver alternated turning on the air-con and opening the windows, I guess in an effort to save fuel. The only issue was every time the windows were opened, clouds of thick red dust came rushing in and choked everyone on the back half of the bus, us included. By 6pm it became clear that we wouldn’t be arriving in Phnom Penh on time, and in the end we stopped off on what appeared to be a random corner a few kilometres outside of Phnom Penh centre at 9pm, two and a half hours late.

The four of us haggled a tuk-tuk driver down to a reasonable fair and we loaded all of our gear including Magnus’ push bike, in to the tiny tuk-tuk and set off for a guesthouse we had been recommended. We attracted quite a lot of attention crammed in to the tuk-tuk with the wheels of the bike hanging out either side.

![](/thebigtrip/assets/images/2010/12/18932339-P1100418.JPG)

Our guesthouse of choice was called Top Banana, but on arrival we found it was fully booked. We hadn’t booked ahead for accommodation for some time but ran out of luck here. Our driver had a list of other places he wanted to try and get us to stay in (I’m guessing for a more healthy commission) so we arranged to return tomorrow when there were rooms available and went off in search of a place just for the one night. After four or five fully booked guesthouses we were starting to wonder if we would be sleeping on the street for the night, but we eventually found one and after checking in we rushed out to get food before everything closed.

**Saturday 11th **December**** The next morning the same tuk-tuk driver (John) picked the four of us up as we had arranged a tour of Phnom Penh. We went first to Top Banana to drop off our bags and had a great breakfast, then set off for the Killing Fields twenty kilometres outside the city centre.

There was an overwhelming sense of loss walking around the mass graves. A stupa had been built within the grounds and housed hundreds of skulls and other bones that had been recovered when the graves were found and excavated. In an effort to save bullets the Khmer Rouge favoured beating people to death. They would then be thrown fully clothed in to large pits, some holding up to four-hundred-and-fifty bodies. As you walked around the site, bits of clothing were still being uncovered by the conveyor belt of tourist’s feet wearing away the earth.

From the Killing Fields we headed back in to Phnom Penh to Tuol Sleng (also known as S21) a former children’s school that the Khmer Rouge converted in to a detention centre for the interrogation of suspects before execution. We wandered the hallways, and looked inside the tiny cells that had been built within the rooms to hold as many people in isolation as possible. There were vivid photographs of the horrors that were committed here just thirty-five years ago. Once we had all gathered outside the last building, we decided it was time to experience a bit of present day Cambodia and headed to the riverfront where the city sits beside the Tonle Sap, just before it converges with the Mekong. We had some food and decided to see one more site before calling it a day.

Wat Phnom is a temple built on the highest point in Phnom Penh. Since it is a largely flat city it was an easy climb up the few sets of stairs to the top though. We met Sambo the elephant who offers rides around the temple grounds, and Jenna fed him the bananas she had been carrying round all day, trying to get us to eat. There were a few monkeys within the grounds as well, taking advantage of any chance to get some food, along with stray cats and dogs, making the place feel a bit like a zoo.

Exhausted we headed back to Top Banana to check out our room. It was huge and although it was called a twin it had two double beds. We unpacked and went to the bar for some drinks where we dangerously opened a tab for our room. Later that evening we went for a walk to find some good Khmer street food. We found a little stall with plastic tables and chairs in a side alley where all the food cost $1. It was great although I’m not entirely sure what some of it was.

That evening we heard there was a band playing at a bar called Equinox, opposite Top Banana. We went with everyone to check them out and had a great night partying along to their funk/Latin jazz/afro beat blend.

**Sunday 12th ****December****** Mira had been staying at Top Banana for a long time and we had been invited to her fancy-dress birthday party tonight; the theme was duck-tape. Recovering slowly from last night we hopped in a tuk-tuk with a few of the guys and went to the old market to buy as much tape as we could find. It turned out we didn’t need to worry as there was a stall selling rolls and rolls of the stuff in various colours. We had a look around before buying what we needed and got lost in the labyrinth of clothes stalls upstairs. The market had almost anything you could have possibly wanted, from car parts to cooking pots, Christmas decorations, electronic goods and building supplies. Not to mention the obvious shoes, souvenirs and jewellery. We picked up some silver, green and pink tape along with some food before returning to the guesthouse to start our outfits.

Locked away in our room we started duck-taping our clothes and an hour later had reasonably respectable outfits. We headed back to the bar and started the party at 3pm. Over the course of the evening loads more people arrived in various outfits and it all got very messy. In the middle of it all we ducked out to see the night market as it was the last time we would get chance before leaving Phnom Penh. We had some food at one of the stalls there as well as picking up a Krama (Cambodian scarf) each.

**Monday 13th ****December****** Back to the working week and it was time for us to sort out our Indian visas. We had to get them in Phnom Penh as it was the only place we would be stationary for long enough before arriving in Delhi. Feeling a little fuzzy we got to the embassy - after a slight mix up with our tuk-tuk driver - with about thirty minutes to spare. There was only one person ahead of us in the queue who wished us good luck as he left. The next thirty minutes were spent handing over our forms only to find something wasn’t quite right and having to amend it. We eventually got everything squared off and with a few minutes to go realised we didn’t have a photocopy of our Cambodian visa. We had all the other paperwork, photocopies of passports and passport photos but the Cambodian visa was also needed. In a fortunate turn of events the hard-faced Indian Embassy officer behind the desk - who had repeatedly told people to go away and come back tomorrow - copied our visas out of our passports and attached them to our applications. We were told to come back on Thursday morning to collect our visas.

It was an exhausting experience and we thought we’d be stuck in Phnom Penh an extra day, but fingers crossed we would collect our visas on Thursday and could leave Friday morning.

We headed back to the guesthouse for some food and to celebrate our applications being processed. Later in the afternoon we dodged all the tuk-tuk drivers waiting outside Top Banana and took a walk up to a book shop to exchange some of our books for fresh reading material. We were both getting sucked in to a couple of storylines and found the next books in each series at D’s Books. It was turning in to a productive day and back at the guesthouse while deciding what to do about an evening meal, one of the guys we met at the duck-tape party dropped by and invited us to a Khmer BBQ some of his friends were having. We jumped at the chance as we had heard great things about Cambodian BBQ food and set off for a big meal with some of his friends.

When we got there we soon found out it was a leaving party for Alex’s friend and housemate, John. There were also some of the band members from Los Poporks who we saw a few nights before, and a gathering of Americans who were living in Cambodia now. At the far end of the table there were four or five Cambodians who knew everyone else through a couple of NGO’s that people in the group were in charge of or working for. In all the table grew to accommodate about twenty-five of us and we had some great food and cheap beer whilst finding out what everyone else was doing in Cambodia. At the end of the evening a small dessert was passed around which we were told was honeycomb. On closer inspection although it was the shape of honeycomb, it was actually bee larvae that had been steamed then covered in butter. Definitely not my favourite dessert; everyone had a little taste before Savoeun - one of the Cambodians at the far end of the table - scooped the rest in to his mouth and sat there with a satisfied grin.

Full of barbequed beef, pork, squid and some amazing corn, we headed back toward the guesthouse, stopping off in Equinox, where the hours rushed passed until it was early morning again. We were all hungry and one of the locals managed to order us burgers from a vendor at the night market who delivered them right to our seats at the bar. Just like a kebab run at the end of a heavy night in the UK.

**Tuesday 14th ****December****** Another late start we decided it was time to see some of the other sights in Phnom Penh. Nobody else was feeling the same so we left them to stew in their hangovers and walked up to the Royal Palace and Silver Pagoda. The grounds for the palace are vast and full of beautiful gardens. I was a bit surprised to find over three-quarters of the grounds closed off to the public, so our ticket only got us in to the main buildings where we could catch glimpses of the gardens surrounding them. We started with a large temple housing hundreds of different sized Buddha’s in various poses; lying down, standing, walking and sitting. From there we were guided by arrows to the walled grounds of the Silver Pagoda where we saw a scaled replica of Angkor Wat and watched a group playing traditional musical instruments. The Silver Pagoda was the highlight, where shoes had to be removed and cameras put away. The funniest bit was nearly all the floor and therefore the silver tiles everyone had come to see, was covered in carpet for protection. We could see small sections of it at the edges of the walkways and took the opportunity to stand barefoot on it when we got the chance. In the centre of the pagoda there were also many sculptured Buddha’s, the two most noteworthy being a life sized replica weighing around ninety kilos in solid gold and adorned with thousands of diamonds and an emerald Buddha sitting high up in a case out of arms reach. On the way out of the palace we saw a video for the temples of Angkor near Siem Reap (our next stop) which got us excited about moving on and seeing the eighth wonder of the world.

The evening was spent trying to re-arrange our flight home and involved repeated calls to Virgin Atlantic, Air New Zealand and eventually our travel agent Trailfinders who were very surprised we were having such difficulty. After explaining how long we had spent trying to change our flights, Trailfinders offered to get involved and speak to both airlines to clear things up. We left them to it and went upstairs to the bar to get some food.

Although the kitchen wasn’t supposed to be closed, Top Banana was down to just one member of staff running the bar. We decided to cross the street to a Thai restaurant called Setsara that offered a set menu worthy of some further investigation. We both had the same thing, vegetable samosas, a chicken stir-fry, prawn red curry and mango in sweet sticky rice for dessert. It was a huge meal and we were glad we only had to make it back across the road to lie down. Just as we were finishing our drinks we heard cracks of thunder and the heavens opened. It was the first rain we had seen since Sydney and it was so thick you could barely see through it. The smell in the air soon changed to ‘wet dog’ and we ran across the road - landing in almost every puddle - to the shelter of our guesthouse.

A quick call back to Trailfinders and they had got the number for an Air New Zealand office in Hong Kong we had to speak to. That would be a job for the morning as the office was now closed.

**Wednesday 15th** ****December**** First thing we made the call to Air NZ in Hong Kong. We got a very helpful person on the other end that could make all the changes we needed (finally!). We asked to amend the date of our flight home and in a few minutes she had organised the flight change with Virgin Atlantic. At the same time we asked to confirm a flight change we had already made with Singapore Airlines from Singapore to Delhi. This had not updated on her record so she would have to call Singapore Airlines and check what they had done. We could then call back later to check the status. With the flights still not completely resolved we headed out to the markets.

We were searching for toys, books and pens as we planned to go to a volunteer school/orphanage and donate the gifts as well as help in the English classes some of the students took. We picked up a bowling set, some rackets and ball along with some coloured card and felt pens. We had some food at the market before finding a tuk-tuk driver who knew the way to the school. At least he said he knew the way to the school. Twenty minutes later we arrived at the wrong school and he checked the address I had written down again before setting off in another direction. This continued for another hour with various stops to ask directions from passers by and staff working at the petrol station. We eventually passed a landmark I recognised and I knew we were close.

Arriving at S.C.A.O some of the kids came running out to see who we were and what we were doing there. We met one of the teachers, some of whom were also living at the orphanage. Her name was Srey Lat and she was in her late teens. She was studying English during the day at university with some of the funding the school received. In the late afternoon she was back to spend the rest of the day until 8pm teaching English to any of the children who would attend. There was a group of Australian school kids who had stopped off to donate bags of clothes they had collected before coming on their school trip, and they got all the local children dancing along to music. We played some football before the next lessons began, and Nat and I decided to stay right through until the end of the lessons to help out any way we could. Over the course of the next few hours we were amazed to see so many children and teenagers arriving for their lessons, enthusiastic and happy to be learning a language that will help them get ahead in a country that is rapidly adopting English as a business language. The other main teacher was named Hom, again no older than late-teens, he was also learning English at university and passing it on as soon as he got home. The last lesson of the day was filled with young teens all practising their English skills and we were asked to help them with their pronunciation. We had to give an introduction of who we were and where we were from; what we did as a job and what we liked at home and in Cambodia. Everyone had to take notes and also had to ask us questions. One of the guys asked Nat if she would cut his hair so she promised to bring her scissors the next day. It was an amazingly rewarding experience and after joining the man behind the whole project for an evening meal, Hom and Srey Lat’s brother gave us a ride back in to Phnom Penh on two scooters. We were really looking forward to going back the next day.

**Thursday 16th ****December****** First thing in the morning we finally confirmed our new itinerary with Air New Zealand. No more flights to worry about! At the visa office we were expecting to pay our money and pick up our Indian visa inserts, only to find that we had to drop-off our passports and pay in the morning slot, then come back in the evening to collect them. This would ruin our plans to spend the rest of the day at S.C.A.O, so we pushed our luck with the same officer knowing from our previous visit that he had a helpful side. He agreed we could come back the next morning to collect our passports even though the mornings were only ever ‘drop-off not pick-up’. We thanked him profusely and left our passports with him hoping security would let us in the following day.

It was still early so we paid a visit to central market; another collection of stalls selling everything you may or may not need, but this time under the cover of a huge meringue shaped and coloured building. We picked up some bits and pieces before meeting Jenna to go to S.C.A.O as she was keen to join us today.

![](/thebigtrip/assets/images/2010/12/18932337-IMG_0903.JPG)

It was a much quicker journey the second time and we spent the rest of the day at S.C.A.O. Nat had a flurry of haircuts to perform as one turned in to three and kept her busy until the evening lessons began. I spent an hour or so helping the kids on two laptops that had been donated by a previous volunteer. It was difficult explaining to them that the keyboards were in German but the language in English so some of the keys didn’t match what appeared on the screen. At the end of the evening we were really sad we couldn’t come back the next day. We headed back to the city centre exhausted and looking forward to some food.

On the way back it started to rain again and just as we got inside the guesthouse another downpour began, this time joined by flashes of lightning and more cracks of thunder. We sat at the bar and caught up with the others on their days events. Sovy the guesthouse owner gave us a free beer each when he saw the size of our tab. It couldn’t be that bad!

**Friday 17th ****December****** The time had finally arrived to collect our visas and it felt like Christmas had come early. Our third visit to the Embassy had us waiting outside as one of the security guards was away getting coffee. On his return we spent a few minutes explaining we had left our passports inside for collection today. He looked at us like we were insane because morning was ‘drop-off not pick-up’, but eventually let us through to go and speak with our now best friend behind the service window. We got a smile and he actually seemed pleased to see us. We picked up our passports and skipped all the way back to the tuk-tuk that returned us safely to Top Banana for breakfast.

We had booked the quarter-to-one bus to Siem Reap and chilled out in the bar with most of the people we had met over the last seven days until we had to leave. I just had time to grab some pies from a shop I had seen below the guesthouse on the day we arrived. It was like a little slice of Australia and was just what was needed before the long bus journey.
