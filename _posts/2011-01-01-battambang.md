title: "Battambang"
date: 2011-01-01 02:32:00

It was an easy three hour journey from Siem Reap to Battambang by Cambodian standards. More than half of the route consisted of proper roads wide enough for vehicles to pass each other without slowing to a crawl.

We were staying in the Royal Hotel on the slightly more crowded west bank of the Stung Sangker River, just behind the main market, Psar Nat. A short walk from the bus drop-off point, we avoided all the tuk-tuk drivers and made our way on foot. The streets looked busy and all the shops spilled out on to the pavements, a common feature of Cambodia, so you have to walk in the road with the rest of the traffic to actually get anywhere.

We checked in and found our room was significantly nicer than we had been staying in recently. For six pounds we got a proper bed cover, comfortable pillows (with a real flower on each welcoming us) and even a TV and dressing table. The room came with air-conditioning and a hot water shower but they cost extra so we would make do with a fan and were by now very used to cold showers. We climbed the stairs to the roof top terrace where we could get some lunch and began writing out twenty-one ‘Christmas’ postcards.

Every time we walked near the hotel reception there were tuk-tuk drivers asking us whether we wanted to go and ride the bamboo train or see the bat cave. We had read in Lonely Planet that the train was worth seeing, but due to the cost of the trip we turned it down. We decided to see as much of Battambang as we could on foot, and be satisfied with that.

We walked north back along the route that brought us in to the city, where we had passed a roundabout with a huge statue of Vishnu at its centre. From there we walked a back road south which would eventually join up with the road to our hotel. There were many little wooden huts selling everything you could get on the main streets of Battambang for a fraction of the price. It was a dusty, busy street with children playing on it and as we walked they would wave and shout hello. It often took that interaction from children to soften the stares you received from their parents as you walked passed.

Back in the city centre we headed further south along the waterfront and found Christmas hats and tinsel that we bought in preparation for meeting Mark and Sam in Koh Chang. That evening we found ventured out to the Smokin’ Pot restaurant recommended in Lonely Planet for some delicious food and a free show, geckos feasting on bugs on a pillar right next to our table.

The next morning after a great breakfast from a small place two doors down from the hotel, we set off in search of the post office to send our twenty-one postcards out in to the world. The waterfront road running alongside the river was without shade and by the time we got to the post office we were thankful for the cool air-conditioning provided while we stuck twenty-one stamps in place. The two women working behind the counter thought it hilarious that we had so many cards to send.

Heading further south, aiming for the taxi rank at the bottom of the city, we stopped in at the Tourist Information Centre, one of the nicest looking buildings in Battambang. Part of a large French colonial building, it was in stark contrast to the dirtier concrete buildings in the city centre. Here we were told we didn’t need to carry out the complicated sequence of individual hops to get to Koh Chang, as there was a bus service running there and we could get a ticket from one of the bus companies on the opposite side of the city. We wanted to get a tuk-tuk across Battambang, but as is always the case when you actually want one, none were around. We walked back, this time one road in from the waterfront where the buildings offered some shade.

Arriving at the ticket office we were told there were no buses to Koh Chang. We would need to get a taxi to the border, and then make our way from there, as we had previously thought. We booked a taxi through the bus company for 9am the next morning before checking out two of the markets Battambang has to offer.

Boeung Chhouk Market spanned two floors and must have been two-hundred metres long. One half was dedicated to clothes, watches, electronics, toys and jewellery, while the other was a high-ceilinged open-plan area dedicated to fifty or sixty butchers stalls. In the heat the smell was almost too much. There were carcasses everywhere and lots of the meat was being swarmed around by flies. We got out of there wondering if all the local restaurants by their supplies there. Outside the main building the market spilled in to the streets, where small stalls sold fruit and spices, dried fish and vegetables. One side was also dedicated to salons and Nat couldn’t resist asking to take a picture of three or four in a row.

![](/thebigtrip/assets/images/2011/01/19359861-P1110039.JPG)

Psar Nat Market is at the heart of Battambang and is made up of two buildings with an open air food court in the centre. It focused on clothes and electronics; knock-off CD’s and DVD’s. We were looking for ‘disco watches’, fake old-school Casio’s that come in almost every colour. We had seen them in Phnom Penh but never made it back to the same market to buy some. We were out of luck in Battambang so would have to keep our fingers crossed that Bangkok wouldn’t disappoint.

We grabbed a late lunch in a small family-run restaurant opposite our hotel where the food was noticeably cheaper and the service noticeably better. The owner brought out free starters as our food took more than five minutes to arrive. After finishing our mains we were also supplied a free dessert of diced fruits; a three course meal for the price of one.

The evening was spent catching up with people on Skype as we had credit left over from sorting our flights in Phnom Penh. Late that night we ventured to the roof terrace for a baguette filled with Dairylea. All the other restaurants were closed.

The next day was going to a solid day of travel. We hoped to make it all the way to Koh Chang in one day to meet up with Mark and Sam, but we had allowed a day in Trat, just before the ferry crossing if we ran out of time.

After breakfast we sat around waiting for the taxi. My Say at the ticket office had said to call if it was any more than ten minutes late. He gave us his number, the taxi driver’s number, his word it would be there on time. It was his reputation at stake. It was a good speech to secure our twelve dollars. We gave the taxi fifteen minutes then put in a call. The English speaking taxi driver played dumb on the phone and hung up. I asked the hotel owner to call Mr Say and see what was going on. All we got in response was the taxi would be here ‘later’. I left Nat with the bags and headed for the ticket office. Mr Say was nowhere to be seen; two other guys there listened to my compliant and smiled. After speaking to Mr Say again on the phone, he eventually crept out from a room behind the desk to meet me. The taxi was late and he was very sorry. It would also be a different driver who spoke no English. It eventually arrived and picked me up, before heading for the hotel and collecting Nat and our bags.

We made it as far as the northern taxi rank before stopping. The taxi would only leave when full; eight people was considered full. There was already a family of three in the car when we got in, but the youngest girl of around eight or nine didn’t count so we needed three more people before our journey would start. Over the next hour and a half, two more women who were heading our way had their bags squashed in to the boot next to ours and waited for the elusive last passenger. It was eleven thirty and we were starting to accept we would not make it to Koh Chang today. A combination of wanting to get moving, and the fact that there was really no way they could squeeze another person in the back of the Toyota Camry, made us pay five dollars to get us on the road and heading for the border.

The front of the car had three passengers and the driver. The back seat had four of us surrounded by bags and bottles that wouldn’t fit in the jam-packed boot. The two hour journey to the border passed relatively quickly. We listened to the women in the front chatting and laughing, occasionally harassing the young man driving as they were as uncomfortable as us in the back. The road started out well, although every few kilometres we stopped to pay a police officer who walked in to our path and stopped us. When the police stops ended, so did the tarmac. From there on we were back to rutted dirt tracks.

![](/thebigtrip/assets/images/2011/01/19359868-P1110041.JPG)

The border crossing was painless, but once on the Thai side we realised we were back in a land with a single currency, and we had none of it. There was no tuk-tuk hassling us for our custom, no taxi drivers trying to remove our bags from our backs and stuff them in their cars. We had crossed from Psar Phrum to Dan Lem, an out of the way border post more than one-hundred kilometres south of the main crossing at Poipet. We found two moto drivers who would take us to a local market to exchange some of our US dollars to Thai baht, before dropping us four kilometres down the road at a sawngthaew stop where we could get a truck to Chanthaburi.

Apart from our crossing by boat from Thailand to Laos, this was our first overland crossing between countries in Southeast Asia. The difference was staggering. The roads were all tarmac; they were curbed and most had pavements. The increase in the number of cars was significant, and the street side shops and stalls were cleaner and better maintained. It made me wish I had somehow absorbed more of the feel Cambodia, even though I had found it at times difficult.

At Chanthaburi we hardly had time to get out of the sawngthaew before the next driver was asking where we wanted to go. We had made better time than expected and could possibly make it to Koh Chang after all. He explained that the ferries to Koh Chang were stopping at 5pm tonight rather than the usual 7pm, but couldn’t explain why. He could take us the whole way for a ‘cheap-cheap price’ and make sure we got to the ferry on time. Knowing full well he was talking rubbish, we managed to haggle him down to a more reasonable price before jumping aboard. There wasn’t anyone else around to share in a ride to Trat and it would save having the same conversation with the next driver if we could just get to the ferry crossing in one hop from here.

He drove like a man possessed in his little truck; music blaring from his speakers in the front, us bouncing around in the back. He got us to the ferry terminal just in time for the 5pm ferry, having stopped off at his friends just outside of Trat to get us a ‘cheap-cheap price’ on ferry tickets. The ferries were running until 7pm as we thought, but we were happy to be on our way before sunset. And as it would turn out, being on this particular ferry would save us (and Mark and Sam) quite a lot of cash.
