Following a much nicer journey on another night bus, we were once again swamped by taxi and bemo drivers all wanting to know where we were going. We had a couple of ideas, but strangely hadn’t really come to a definite conclusion on the eighteen hour bus ride from Yogyakarta. We took some small delight in making them all wait for us to decide where we wanted to go and in the end only two were patient enough to hang around until we decided Kuta would be worth a look as it was the closest.

We were dropped at the beach end of a little lane called ‘Poppies Gang One’; one of two narrow passages joining the main beach road and the main shopping street some five-hundred metres inland. The lane was just wide enough for a small car to fit down (which they frequently did, beeping at pedestrians) and had high concrete walls either side when there were not shops spilling out on to the walkway. It was like navigating a maze.

We went to a few ‘losmen’ style rooms recommended in LP and found most were either full or had massively inflated prices over what the book quoted. We decided it would be better searching once we had some breakfast, so dumped our bags at a table in a large but empty dining area and had a bite to eat. Feeling refreshed we went back to hunting down a bed and decided it would be worth looking in on a few of the rooms offered by people lurking on the street. We were shown to a little courtyard surrounded by rooms and run by a local family, where we managed to get a good price with our best haggling skills.

Kuta was already proving to be a bit of a headache and the first place in Indonesia we had seen where tourists so easily outnumbered locals. That also made the prices much higher than we were expecting. We spent an hour or so on the beach (which wasn’t that great) before the constant interruptions from people trying to sell you a hat, sarong, neck rest, some fruit, a massage or a cheap tattoo made us retreat back to our room. We ventured out later for food and had a better look around, seeing the massive amount of development happening on the beach road, with huge areas fenced off and big artist’s impressions showing what the newest hotel will look like on completion. It wasn’t until 11pm that evening that we found out it was actually midnight. Crossing from Java to Bali we went forward an hour but the guys on the bus didn’t mention anything and we hadn’t looked in to it.

The next morning we grabbed some breakfast and booked a shuttle bus to Ubud for early afternoon. It was to be another tourist hotspot but we’d heard it had a much nicer atmosphere and a real slice of Balinese culture.

We were dropped at the south end of ‘Monkey Forest Road’ (not its official name but a widely adopted pseudonym). We walked north towards the collection of homestays we had reviews for, looking in to all the little cafés and restaurants, bookshops and spas along the way. Checking out our first option, Frog Pond Inn, we found the price over double what we were expecting with no room for negotiation. The next stop, Mandia Bungalows was over three times what the Lonely Planet had suggested it would be. So much for the prices being accurate after all.

A little distressed we continued on and bumped in to a guy on the street asking if we needed a room. Carrying backpacks always attracts attention from people looking to fill rooms. We asked upfront what the price would be and Nat got him to agree to quite a reduction for two nights, including breakfast. It had to be worth a look. We followed this young guy, who we would later find out was nicknamed Blue (his real name was Wayan, but if you find the time to look in to Balinese naming traditions you will soon find out why everyone has nicknames) down a long winding alleyway and through a small door in to a courtyard. Here we met his Uncle - the owner of the guesthouse - and a huge array of cousins, aunties and grandparents. They all lived on the bottom floor of this building and the guestrooms were on the first floor. It was a hive of activity as there was a grand ceremony the following day which required a great deal of organisation; preparing offerings and ceremonial outfits, adorning statues and shrines. The room was really quaint and there was a terrace with a table and chairs where we could sit and look out at the rice paddies surrounding the area. It was the kind of place that should be in the Lonely Planet but if it was would be triple the price and always fully booked. Oh and the breakfasts were awesome; omelette and toast or banana pancakes!

Over the next few days we checked out most of the shops, scoured the area for the best value places to eat, and went on a nice walk, out the top of the main village and through field after field of rice paddies, getting a bit lost before finally finding our way back in through the bottom of town, past the monkey sanctuary that gives the main street its nickname. Ubud had so much to offer (Balinese cookery classes, yoga classes, art classes, temples to visit) that we could have stayed much longer, but with one eye on our booked and paid for flight back to Perth, we decided after three days we should move on to see Lombok and the Gili Islands. We booked a shuttle bus to Padang Bai (a small town on Bali’s east coast with a ferry service to Lombok and the Gilis) for the next day and on our last night in Ubud grabbed takeaway Indonesian food from a warung (food stall or small cookhouse) that Blue recommended. It was incredible and our cheapest meal yet.

The shuttle bus dropped us in the heart of Padang Bai and we executed the now very familiar routine of traipsing around for twenty to thirty minutes trying to find a good deal on a room. We planned to spend the night and get the slow (and very cheap) ferry to Lombok the next day. Without hardly trying - more just acting a little indifferent as we inched toward the door - we managed to get a great price on a room that also came with breakfast. Each meal in Indonesia (especially if you ate Western food) could cost as much as your room for the night, so breakfast included was always a bonus.

We soon discovered an issue with our plan when we went to buy tickets for the ferry. The car ferry from Bali to Lombok took four hours and docked at a port in Lembar, two hours south of our intended destination, Sengiggi. The very cheap tickets we had our eyes on, got us on the ferry as foot passengers, which meant we would need transport the other side. That didn’t seem like an issue until it was explained to us that Lombok, like Java and Sumatra is a predominantly Muslim country (unlike Bali which is primarily Hindu) and for the next two days - in the middle of Ramadan - all Muslims celebrate ‘Idul Fitri’ and nobody goes to work. No shuttle drivers, bus drivers or taxi drivers. There would be no public transport for two days! We had already cut back on our plan of seeing Flores and Komodo due to time and money constraints so we really didn’t want to miss out on Lombok as well, but we were running out of time.

After careful consideration of just what we wanted from our last few days in Indonesia (some time relaxing on a decent beach), we decided we would stump up the extra cash to get the ‘fast’ (and more expensive) boat direct from Padang Bai to the Gili Islands and have five days in one place, with not a lot to do and plenty of time to do it. Every day of travelling seems to cost triple a day stood still, so we thought five days of laying on a beach would also even out the pricey tickets. Haggling for the tickets we also saved some cash. When the agent found out we got the last two places on the boat she wanted us to pay full price again, but we argued she had already agreed a lower rate and should honour it, which after asking for our money back if she wouldn’t, she eventually did.

In the middle of all this plan changing we found the sole ATM in Padang Bai only worked for Indonesian banks and accounts. We had also read there were no ATMs on Gili Trawangan, so we needed cash for the next five days. It turned out the next nearest ATM was about fifteen kilometres away in a scary sounding place by the name of Klungkung. We hired a scooter for the day for about the same price it costs to get your cash out of one of those pesky ATMs in bars in the UK that charge you a withdrawal fee, and set off in search of Klungkung. We had to buy petrol on the way which was for sale in scooter sized quantities in water bottles stacked in a re-purposed chicken hutch on the side of the main road out of Padang Bai. Having experienced the chaos of Indonesia’s roads from the relative safety of a big solid coach, it now felt like we were flying through an asteroid field of other scooters, cars and trucks. Undertaking, overtaking, indicating, not indicating; every manoeuvre is considered perfectly respectable as long as you beep your horn lots first.

Money sorted, tickets sorted, new plan ready for action, we stayed another night in Padang Bai and looked forward to our now very quick journey to Gili Trawangan the following morning.
